Domestic Airlines — Hot Weather Operations
Lesson 3: Fuel in Hot Weather

Learning objectives:
- Explain the effect of temperature on fuel density and quantity.
- Apply fuel checks and MEL fuel items.
- Use the operational flight plan fuel figures correctly.

Fuel density

Hot fuel is less dense : the same mass takes up more volume. Uplift is often measured in litres but planned in kilograms — always convert with the actual density on the fuel docket.

Check the uplift : the difference between the calculated and the actual uplift must be within the company tolerance.

Respect any maximum fuel temperature limit in the AFM.

Fuel planning

Use the Jetplan figures: trip fuel, contingency, alternate, final reserve, taxi and any extra fuel. Check the effect of a change of cruise level or take-off mass.

Hot weather may impose a lower cruise level or a longer climb. Plan for possible weather deviations around convective activity.

Check the zero fuel weight and fuel entries carefully — incorrect ZFW or fuel entries are a recurring flight safety event (Lesson 5).

Fuel system and MEL

B737-800: perform the spar fuel shut-off valve check when required (FCOM SP 12-01).

Read MEL fuel items in full, including the (M) maintenance and (O) operational procedures.

Example from the company LOFT — B737-800, MEL 28-01-03-01
A main tank aft fuel boost pump may be inoperative provided that: (a) both main tank forward pumps operate normally; (b) at the start of take-off, the fuel quantity in the associated tank is not less than 7,500 lb (3,402 kg); (c) a minimum fuel quantity of 2,500 lb (1,134 kg) is maintained in the associated tank; and (d) the boost pump is deactivated.

Always use the current MEL — this example is for training only.

Key takeaways:
- Hot fuel is less dense — convert volume to mass with the actual density.
- Check the uplift and the ZFW / fuel entries carefully.
- Read every MEL item in full, including (M) and (O) procedures.
