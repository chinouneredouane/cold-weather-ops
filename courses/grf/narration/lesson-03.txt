Domestic Airlines — Global Reporting Format (GRF)
Lesson 3: Runway Surface States and Contaminant Identification

Learning objectives:
- Define the four runway surface states.
- Identify the eight contaminant descriptors from observations and photos.
- Explain the impact of the GRF on runway inspection work.

Four runway surface states

Tap a card to turn it over.
DRY Sèche A runway clear of contaminants and visible moisture within the required length and width. WET Mouillée Surface covered by any visible dampness or water up to and including 3 mm . A damp runway is reported WET. SLIPPERY WET Mouillée glissante A wet runway whose surface friction is degraded — rubber deposits in the touchdown zone, poor texture, poor drainage. Identified by measurements, prior methods or AIREPs. CONTAMINATED Contaminée A significant part of the surface (more than 25 % of a third in use) covered by one or more contaminant descriptors.

Fluid and hard contaminants

Eight descriptors exist (Annex 1 of EU 139/2014). They can be combined to describe the surface — for example dry snow on top of ice.

Tap a card to turn it over.
STANDING WATER Eau stagnante — fluid Water deeper than 3 mm . Often only in puddles: check the coverage. SLUSH Neige fondante — fluid Water-saturated snow: water runs when you squeeze it and splashes when you step on it. Typically 0 to +5 °C. WET SNOW Neige mouillée — fluid Snow with liquid water: it forms a compact ball, but no water runs out. Not to be confused with slush. DRY SNOW Neige sèche — fluid Falls in cold weather (around −5 °C or below). Light flakes that do not stick: it will not form a ball. COMPACTED SNOW Neige compactée — hard Snow compressed into a solid mass: a vehicle does not sink in; it breaks into lumps when picked up. FROST Gelée / givre — hard Ice crystals formed from airborne moisture on a surface below 0 °C: granular texture, often after calm, clear nights. ICE Glace — hard Frozen water: thicker than frost, very slippery, not shiny; removal is difficult. WET ICE Glace mouillée — hard Ice with water on top, or melting ice — often after freezing rain when the temperature rises above 0 °C. Shiny and wet.

Impact on the aerodrome operator

Before : in case of doubt a friction measurement or decelerometer was used. Now : the surface is described precisely with predefined codes and terms; friction values are not reported.

The operator issues the NOTAM / SNOWTAM, monitors the runway and performs as many inspections as necessary (ADR.OPS.B.037).

Contamination is no longer only a winter problem: an RWYCC and an RCR are published all year .

New procedures, new training, consistency between reality and what is published, consistency between actors, reliable exchanges with ATS (AIREP, RCR) and AIS (NOTAM, SNOWTAM).

Decision aids Use the meteorological data and the reports of previous inspections to identify the contaminant. Pilots should know these descriptors: they are the words of the RCR.

Key takeaways:
- Four states: DRY, WET (≤ 3 mm), SLIPPERY WET, CONTAMINATED.
- Eight descriptors: standing water, slush, wet snow, dry snow, compacted snow, frost, ice, wet ice — can be combined.
- Identification uses observation, temperature and the history of the day.
