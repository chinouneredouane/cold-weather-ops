Domestic Airlines — Upset Prevention and Recovery
Lesson 27: Threat and Error Management, Workload and Fatigue

Learning objectives:
- Apply the threat and error management (TEM) framework to upset prevention.
- Explain how human information processing, fixation and distraction lead to upsets.
- Manage workload and fatigue to keep monitoring effective.

Human information processing

Pilots perceive, interpret, decide and act with a limited attention . Under high workload or stress, attention narrows.

Expectation shapes perception: we tend to see what we expect, and to reject information that does not fit our mental model.

Fixation on one problem, distraction by another task, or simple inattention let a deviation grow unnoticed — the start of most upsets.

Instrument interpretation — read the attitude first: the horizon line and the bank pointer ("sky pointer") show which way is up. Confirm with the other instruments.

Threat and error management (TEM)

Element Definition Upset examples

Threats Events outside the crew's control that increase complexity Turbulence, icing, convective weather, system failures, late ATC changes

Errors Crew actions or inactions that deviate from intentions or expectations Wrong mode selected, speed not monitored, wrong trim, missed callout

Undesired aircraft states Deviations of position, speed, attitude or configuration caused by threats or errors Low speed, excessive bank, unstable approach — the step just before an upset

Countermeasures: anticipate threats in the briefing, detect errors with active monitoring and cross-checks, and recover from an undesired state before it becomes an upset.

Active monitoring and verification

Monitoring is an active task: the PM checks the flight path and FMA, compares with expectations and speaks up (Lesson 15).

Verify every change: mode, target, configuration. "What is it doing now? What will it do next?"

Workload management

Plan high-workload phases in advance; brief the threats.

Prioritise: aviate, navigate, communicate . Delegate tasks clearly between PF and PM.

Choose the level of automation that reduces workload — and revert to manual flight when the automation does not do what is needed.

Fatigue

Fatigue slows reactions, narrows attention and increases errors — including missed monitoring.

Use the company fatigue management and reporting procedures (OM A FTL chapter). Tell the other pilot when you are tired, and use controlled rest only as the OM allows.

Crew resource management

All these skills come together in CRM: a shared mental model, clear communication, assertiveness and leadership. Most upset accidents show a breakdown of at least one of them.

Key takeaways:
- Limited attention, expectation, fixation and distraction let deviations grow.
- TEM: threats → errors → undesired aircraft states → upset; anticipate, detect, recover.
- Active monitoring and verification of every mode and target change.
- Manage workload (aviate, navigate, communicate) and report fatigue.
