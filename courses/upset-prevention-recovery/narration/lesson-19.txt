Domestic Airlines — Upset Prevention and Recovery
Lesson 19: Upset Recovery Principles

Learning objectives:
- State the general principles of upset recovery.
- Explain why stall recovery takes priority.
- Describe the correct use of automation, thrust, rudder and g during recovery.

The recovery sequence

Recognise and confirm the situation — attitude first, cross-checked (Lesson 17).

Disconnect the autopilot and autothrust — they may be causing or worsening the upset, and their behaviour is unpredictable at extreme attitudes.

If stalled, recover from the stall first — reduce angle of attack (Lesson 20). Attitude cannot be recovered while the wing is stalled.

Recover the attitude using the nose-high or nose-low technique (Lessons 21 and 22).

Return to a safe flight path — level flight or a climb, appropriate speed, trimmed.

General principles

Use the manufacturer's procedures. The generic principles here explain why ; the FCOM and QRH tell you how for your type.

Pitch is controlled with elevator and stabiliser trim. Use trim carefully to assist a large pitch input, not as the primary control.

Roll with aileron and spoiler. Full deflection may be needed. Rudder is normally not required and must never be used in rapid alternating inputs.

Thrust is used as required — remember pitch-power coupling: reducing thrust can help lower the nose, adding thrust helps a nose-low recovery only after the wings are level and the speed allows it.

Respect g limits. Unload in a nose-high recovery; avoid a rolling pull and excessive g in a nose-low recovery.

Do not change configuration (flaps, gear) unless the procedure requires it. Speedbrakes may be used in high-speed nose-low situations.

Communicate — announce the upset and the recovery; the pilot monitoring calls out attitude, speed and altitude.

Common errors

Pulling back in a stall or a nose-low, high-bank attitude.

Applying full thrust in a nose-high, low-speed upset on an aircraft with underwing engines.

Over-controlling — large inputs in both directions, especially at high altitude.

Using rudder to roll, or making alternating rudder inputs.

Failing to disconnect the automation, or re-engaging it too early.

Key takeaways:
- Recognise and confirm → automation off → stall recovery first → attitude recovery → safe flight path.
- Elevator and trim for pitch; aileron and spoiler for roll; rudder normally not needed.
- Use thrust as required, with pitch-power coupling in mind.
- Communicate throughout; follow the manufacturer's procedures.
