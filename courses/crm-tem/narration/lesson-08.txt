Domestic Airlines — CRM/TEM — Initial and Recurrent
Lesson 8: Situation Awareness, Information Processing and Workload

Learning objectives:
- Define situation awareness and its three levels.
- Describe how information is acquired and processed.
- Apply workload management.

The CRM skills

Situation awareness

Tap a card to turn it over.
Level 1 — Perceive Gather the relevant information: instruments, outside, radio, crew, cabin. Level 2 — Understand Give it meaning: what does this mean for my flight? Level 3 — Project Anticipate: what will happen next, and what will I do if…? Loss of SA warning signs Ambiguity, confusion, fixation, nobody flying, deviations, unresolved discrepancies, "what is it doing now?".

The best situation awareness keeps the smallest gap between the crew's picture and reality. The NOTECHS elements are: awareness of aircraft systems, of the external environment, and of time .

Information acquisition and processing

Attention is limited: we sample information, and under stress the sampling narrows.

Expectation shapes perception: we hear the clearance we expect, and see the runway we expect.

Verbalise: thinking aloud lets the other pilot check your picture ("verbalise, verify, monitor").

Use "What if…?" to keep projecting ahead.

Workload management

Priorities do not change: aviate, navigate, communicate, manage systems . Plan the high-workload phases early (briefings before top of descent), delegate tasks, use the automation level that reduces workload, and say when you are saturated. Avoid task fixation: one pilot always flies the aircraft.

Under-load Low workload is also a threat: vigilance drops on long cruise phases at night. Keep monitoring routines and planned cross-checks.

Key takeaways:
- SA: perceive, understand, project.
- Awareness of systems, environment and time.
- Aviate, navigate, communicate — plan, delegate, avoid fixation.
